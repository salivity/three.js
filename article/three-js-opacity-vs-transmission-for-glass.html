Three.js Opacity vs Transmission for Glass

When rendering glass in Three.js, developers must choose between Material.opacity and Material.transmission. While opacity uniformly fades an object into the background using standard alpha blending, transmission physically simulates light passing through a solid transparent volume, enabling realistic refraction, surface scattering, and Fresnel reflections. Understanding the operational and visual differences between these two properties is critical for achieving authentic glass shaders without unnecessary performance costs.

What is Material.opacity?

Material.opacity controls traditional alpha blending. When you lower an object's opacity (with transparent: true), Three.js renders the surface by blending the fragment's output color with the colors of the objects already drawn behind it in the frame buffer.

Key characteristics of opacity:

  • No Refraction: Light travels straight through the surface without bending. Objects behind the glass appear in their exact physical positions without distortion.
  • Surface-Level Fading: Both the diffuse light and the specular highlights (reflections) fade equally as opacity decreases. Real glass maintains bright, crisp reflections even when the body of the material is completely clear.
  • Depth Sorting Issues: Standard transparency requires Three.js to sort objects from back to front before rendering. Complex overlapping transparent geometries often suffer from sorting artifacts, missing faces, or incorrect draw orders.

What is Material.transmission?

Material.transmission is exclusive to MeshPhysicalMaterial (an extension of MeshStandardMaterial). It represents the percentage of light that penetrates the surface and travels through the medium rather than being reflected or absorbed.

Key characteristics of transmission:

  • Physical Refraction: Transmissive materials use an Index of Refraction (ior, typically set to 1.5 for standard glass) to bend the light rays passing through the mesh, distorting the scene behind it based on the geometry’s thickness and curvature.
  • Independent Specular Reflection: Even when transmission is set to 1.0 (fully transmissive), the surface retains strong Fresnel reflections at glancing angles. The reflections remain opaque while the transmitted light shows the refracted background.
  • Roughness Scattering: The material’s roughness property directly blurs the refracted background, effortlessly creating frosted glass or frosted acrylic effects. With opacity, roughness only affects surface highlights, leaving the background perfectly sharp.
  • Thickness and Attenuation: Used in conjunction with properties like thickness, attenuationColor, and attenuationDistance, transmission simulates colored or dense glass by absorbing light as it travels deeper through the object.

Performance and Pipeline Differences

The visual realism of transmission comes with a rendering cost:

  1. Render Targets: To bend and blur the background, Three.js must first render the opaque background objects to an internal texture, then pass that texture to the transmissive material's shader to sample it. This extra render pass can impact performance on low-end or mobile devices.
  2. Double-Sided Limitations: Transmissive objects primarily calculate refraction using a screen-space approximation based on the surface facing the camera. Rendering thick, complex interiors or nested glass meshes can sometimes exhibit visual inaccuracies compared to true ray-traced glass.

Summary: When to Use Which

  • Use opacity for transparent UI elements, simple colored plastics, fading objects in and out, or performance-critical scenes on low-power devices where refraction is not required.
  • Use transmission whenever you need to simulate realistic glass, water, crystals, or gems where physical light bending, realistic Fresnel reflections, and blurred background transmissions are essential.
