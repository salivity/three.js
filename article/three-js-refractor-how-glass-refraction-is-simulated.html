Three.js Refractor: How Glass Refraction is Simulated

The Refractor class in Three.js simulates the bending of light through transparent surfaces by combining dynamic off-screen rendering, projective texture mapping, and shader-based distortion. Rather than tracing actual light rays through volumetric geometry, this utility component captures the scene from a specialized perspective, renders that view to a texture, and displaces the texture coordinates across the mesh surface to create the visual illusion of optical refraction.

Virtual Camera and Off-Screen Rendering

At the core of the Refractor class is a dynamic rendering pipeline that relies on a secondary, virtual camera and an off-screen render target (WebGLRenderTarget).

When the main scene renders, the Refractor calculates the world position and orientation of the mesh acting as the glass interface. It positions the virtual camera to match the viewpoint of the active scene camera. Before the main scene is drawn to the screen, the Refractor temporarily hides its host mesh and renders the background objects into its internal render target. This generates a live 2D image of what lies behind the refractive object.

Oblique Clipping Planes

To ensure that objects located between the camera and the glass surface do not appear inside the refracted image, the Refractor uses an oblique near-clipping plane.

The class constructs a mathematical plane aligned precisely with the surface of the geometry. It transforms this plane into camera space and modifies the virtual camera’s projection matrix using an oblique projection technique. This clips out any geometry located on the viewer’s side of the glass, ensuring the off-screen render target records only the environment located physically behind the refractor plane.

Projective Texture Mapping

Once the background scene is captured into the render target, the Refractor applies this texture to the mesh using projective texture coordinates.

In standard texturing, UV coordinates are mapped statically to geometry vertices. In contrast, the Refractor's custom vertex shader calculates texture coordinates based on the clip-space position of the vertices relative to the main camera. This aligns the rendered background texture with the viewer's line of sight, making the glass mesh look initially transparent and seamlessly integrated into the environment.

Simulating Snell's Law via Shader Distortion

True physical refraction follows Snell’s law, where the change in the angle of light depends on the refractive indices of two media. The Refractor approximates this behavior inside its fragment shader.

Instead of computing complex multi-bounce volumetric paths, the shader offsets the projective texture coordinates before sampling the background texture:

  1. Normal Perturbation: Surface normals are calculated across the mesh, often modulated by a normal map to simulate surface roughness, ripples, or curved bevels.
  2. View Vector Calculation: The shader computes the incident angle between the camera's view direction and the surface normal.
  3. Coordinate Displacement: The incident vector and surface normal are used to generate an offset vector. This offset shifts the UV coordinates used to look up pixels in the off-screen render target.

Areas where the surface angles change sharply experience greater UV displacement, distorting the background image and mimicking the optical magnification and bending characteristic of glass.

Planar Approximation and Performance

The Refractor is fundamentally a planar, screen-space approximation. It works best on flat surfaces such as windows, mirrors, water sheets, or thin glass panes. Because it utilizes a single secondary render pass per frame rather than computationally expensive path tracing or bidirectional ray marching, it achieves interactive frame rates in real-time WebGL applications while providing a convincing visual representation of light bending.
