Fix Peter Panning with Three.js Shadow Bias

This article explains how to eliminate "Peter Panning"—a visual artifact where shadows appear detached from their casting objects—when using a DirectionalLight in Three.js. You will learn how shadow bias works, why high bias values detach shadows on floating geometry, and how to combine bias, normalBias, and shadow frustum adjustments to produce grounded, accurate shadows without introducing shadow acne.

Understanding Peter Panning

Peter Panning occurs when the shadow map depth comparison is pushed too far away from the surface casting the shadow. In Three.js, developers frequently increase light.shadow.bias to eliminate "shadow acne" (false self-shadowing moiré patterns). However, setting light.shadow.bias too high creates a gap between the base of an object and where its shadow begins. For floating geometry, this creates unnatural gaps or distorts intended contact shadows on surrounding surfaces.

Step 1: Use normalBias Instead of High bias

The most effective way to prevent Peter Panning without reintroducing shadow acne is to prioritize light.shadow.normalBias over standard light.shadow.bias.

  • light.shadow.bias offsets depth along the light direction. High values directly cause shadow detachment.
  • light.shadow.normalBias offsets geometry along surface normals during the shadow pass, effectively solving self-shadowing on curved or angled surfaces without pushing the shadow away from the base.

Set your standard bias to a very low value (or zero), and dial in a small positive normalBias:

const light = new THREE.DirectionalLight(0xffffff, 1);
light.castShadow = true;

// Keep depth bias minimal
light.shadow.bias = -0.0001;

// Use normalBias to resolve shadow acne without causing Peter Panning
light.shadow.normalBias = 0.02;

Depending on the scale of your scene, normalBias typically works best between 0.01 and 0.05.

Step 2: Tighten the Shadow Camera Frustum

Shadow map texels that cover too much world space exacerbate depth-precision issues. A DirectionalLight uses an orthographic camera for shadows. If this bounding box is excessively large, depth resolution drops, forcing you to use larger bias values.

Fit the shadow camera tightly around the playable or visible scene:

light.shadow.camera.near = 0.5;
light.shadow.camera.far = 50;

light.shadow.camera.left = -10;
light.shadow.camera.right = 10;
light.shadow.camera.top = 10;
light.shadow.camera.bottom = -10;

// Update the projection matrix after changes
light.shadow.camera.updateProjectionMatrix();

Keep the near plane as far forward as possible and the far plane as close as possible to maximize 24-bit depth buffer precision.

Step 3: Increase Shadow Map Resolution

If fine details on floating meshes continue to show gaps, increase the shadow map resolution to increase texel density:

light.shadow.mapSize.width = 2048;
light.shadow.mapSize.height = 2048;

Higher resolution allows you to reduce both bias and normalBias to microscopic values without triggering acne.

Recommended Configuration Template

const directionalLight = new THREE.DirectionalLight(0xffffff, 1.5);
directionalLight.position.set(15, 25, 15);
directionalLight.castShadow = true;

// Shadow Map Resolution
directionalLight.shadow.mapSize.width = 2048;
directionalLight.shadow.mapSize.height = 2048;

// Frustum Bounds
directionalLight.shadow.camera.near = 1;
directionalLight.shadow.camera.far = 60;
directionalLight.shadow.camera.left = -15;
directionalLight.shadow.camera.right = 15;
directionalLight.shadow.camera.top = 15;
directionalLight.shadow.camera.bottom = -15;

// Bias Settings
directionalLight.shadow.bias = -0.00005;
directionalLight.shadow.normalBias = 0.03;

scene.add(directionalLight);

Using a slight negative bias paired with a modest normalBias ensures that shadows stay tightly anchored to the geometry, eliminating Peter Panning while keeping surface rendering free of artifacts.
