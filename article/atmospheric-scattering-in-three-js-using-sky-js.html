Atmospheric Scattering in Three.js Using Sky.js

This article explains how the Three.js Sky.js shader module generates photorealistic skies by simulating Rayleigh and Mie light scattering. It covers the physical principles behind these two scattering models, how they are translated into GLSL shaders, and how runtime parameters like turbidity, sun position, and scattering coefficients determine the visual appearance of the atmosphere from midday blue to sunset red.

The Physics of Atmospheric Scattering

When sunlight enters a planetary atmosphere, photons interact with particles suspended in the air. The visual appearance of the sky is predominantly dictated by two physical processes:

  1. Rayleigh Scattering: Occurs when light interacts with particles much smaller than its wavelength, primarily nitrogen and oxygen molecules. The scattering intensity is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength (\(\lambda^{-4}\)). Consequently, shorter wavelengths (blue light) scatter roughly ten times more efficiently than longer wavelengths (red light). During midday, scattered blue light floods the sky dome. At sunrise or sunset, sunlight travels through a thicker column of air, scattering out almost all short wavelengths before reaching the observer and leaving only reds, oranges, and deep yellows.

  2. Mie Scattering: Occurs when light collides with particles comparable to or larger than its wavelength, such as water droplets, dust, smoke, and pollen (aerosols). Mie scattering is predominantly wavelength-independent, causing all colors to scatter relatively equally and producing a whitish haze. It is also highly directional, creating strong forward scattering that manifests as a distinct bright halo around the sun disk.

How Sky.js Implements the Model

Sky.js is an official Three.js add-on based on analytical sky models (primarily derived from the Preetham and Bruneton/Nishita atmospheric scattering approximations). Instead of simulating millions of individual volumetric air slices in real time—which is computationally prohibitive—Sky.js executes an analytical solution entirely within a WebGL fragment shader mapped across a massive geometric sky dome.

1. Geometry Setup

Sky.js creates an internal Mesh utilizing a large bounding geometry, typically a cube or sphere scaled to encompass the entire camera view range. The inside faces are rendered, ensuring that regardless of camera position or rotation, every viewing ray intersects the atmospheric dome.

2. Optical Depth and Ray Marching Approximations

To compute the color of any pixel on the sky dome, the fragment shader determines a viewing ray vector originating from the camera through the pixel, alongside a directional vector pointing toward the sun. The shader calculates:

  • The optical path length through the atmospheric boundary layer.
  • The scattering angle (\(\theta\)), which is the angle between the camera view vector and the sun vector.

3. Applying the Phase Functions in GLSL

The shader calculates the intensity and color of light arriving at the camera using two specialized phase functions:

  • The Rayleigh Phase Function: Models symmetric forward and backward scattering with uniform circular distribution: \[P_R(\theta) = \frac{3}{16\pi} (1 + \cos^2\theta)\] The shader applies this function scaled by the inverse fourth power of the RGB wavelengths. This colors the general expanse of the sky based on optical path length.

  • The Mie Phase Function (Henyey-Greenstein Approximation): Models asymmetric forward scattering to generate the solar glow: \[P_M(\theta) = \frac{1 - g^2}{4\pi (1 + g^2 - 2g \cos\theta)^{3/2}}\] The asymmetry factor \(g\) (mieDirectionalG) dictates how tightly light scatters forward. A value close to 1.0 produces a sharp, localized sun disk, while lower values produce a broad, hazy glare.

4. Integration of Configurable Uniforms

Sky.js exposes several uniforms to JavaScript that allow real-time control over the fragment shader's variables:

  • sunPosition (Vector3): Controls the trajectory and altitude of the sun. As the sun lowers toward the horizon, the optical thickness increases exponentially, automatically shifting the sky color from pale blue to sunset crimson.
  • rayleigh (Float): Directly scales the strength of molecular scattering. Low values remove the blue hue and turn the sky black (simulating an airless moon environment), while higher values intensify the sky color.
  • turbidity (Float): Represents the concentration of aerosol particles. Higher turbidity increases Mie scattering and shifts the atmosphere from clear blue to an overcast or smog-filled appearance.
  • mieCoefficient (Float): Determines the overall amount of Mie scattering present in the volume.
  • mieDirectionalG (Float): Controls the directionality of the sun’s halo via the Henyey-Greenstein function.

Through this combination of analytical phase calculations and hardware-accelerated fragment shading, Sky.js provides dynamic, physically grounded atmospheric visuals with negligible CPU overhead.
