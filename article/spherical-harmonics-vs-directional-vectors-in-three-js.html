Spherical Harmonics vs Directional Vectors in Three.js

This article explains the mathematical differences between standard directional vectors and spherical harmonics when lighting a Three.js scene. While standard directional vectors use discrete geometric rays to calculate direct specular and diffuse illumination via dot products, spherical harmonics project continuous environmental lighting into a frequency-space polynomial basis. Below, we break down their mathematical formulations, coordinate representations, and how each method solves the rendering equation in real-time WebGL applications.

1. Mathematical Representation

A directional vector in Three.js (THREE.DirectionalLight) represents a single, infinitely distant light source. Mathematically, it is defined as a discrete unit vector on the unit sphere \(S^2\):

\[\mathbf{L} = (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \quad \text{where} \quad \|\mathbf{L}\| = 1\]

The incident radiance \(L_i(\mathbf{\omega})\) from a directional light is modeled as a Dirac delta function centered at direction \(\mathbf{L}\):

\[L_i(\mathbf{\omega}) = E_{\text{light}} \, \delta(\mathbf{\omega} - \mathbf{L})\]

where \(E_{\text{light}}\) is the color and intensity of the light source.

Spherical harmonics (used in THREE.LightProbe and THREE.SphericalHarmonics3) represent continuous functions over the sphere \(S^2\). Instead of a single vector, a spherical harmonic expansion models an entire spherical irradiance field as a linear combination of orthonormal basis functions \(Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)\), analogous to a Fourier transform mapped onto a sphere:

\[f(\theta, \phi) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m=-l}^{l} c_l^m Y_l^m(\theta, \phi)\]

In Three.js, this infinite series is truncated to order \(l = 2\) (3 bands), yielding 9 basis functions (\(l=0\): 1 term, \(l=1\): 3 terms, \(l=2\): 5 terms). Because light has color, each coefficient \(c_l^m\) contains an RGB vector, resulting in 27 scalar values (\(9 \times 3\)) to define the lighting environment.

2. Solving the Rendering Equation

The rendering equation for diffuse Lambertian surfaces requires integrating incoming radiance over the hemisphere \(\Omega\):

\[E(\mathbf{N}) = \int_{\Omega} L_i(\mathbf{\omega}) (\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{\omega}) \, d\mathbf{\omega}\]

Directional Vectors (Discrete Evaluation)

Because a directional light uses a Dirac delta distribution, the integral collapses into a single direct evaluation:

\[E(\mathbf{N}) = E_{\text{light}} \max(0, \mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{L})\]

In shader code, this requires only a standard Euclidean dot product between the normalized surface normal \(\mathbf{N}\) and the light direction \(\mathbf{L}\).

Spherical Harmonics (Analytic Convolution)

Spherical harmonics solve the entire hemispherical convolution analytically. By projecting both the incoming radiance function and the clamped cosine kernel \((\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{\omega})\) into the spherical harmonic basis, the convolution in spatial domain becomes an efficient coefficient multiplication in the frequency domain (the Funk-Hecke theorem).

The diffuse irradiance \(E(\mathbf{N})\) at any normal direction \(\mathbf{N} = (x, y, z)\) is evaluated using a quadratic polynomial derived from the 9 basis functions:

\[E(\mathbf{N}) = c_0 + c_1 y + c_2 z + c_3 x + c_4 x y + c_5 y z + c_6 (3z^2 - 1) + c_7 x z + c_8 (x^2 - y^2)\]

Where \(c_0\) through \(c_8\) incorporate both the scene's projected light coefficients and the cosine lobe expansion coefficients.

3. Key Differences Summary

Property Standard Directional Vector Spherical Harmonics (Order 2)
Data Structure Single Vector3 + color (6 floats) Array of 9 Vector3 coefficients (27 floats)
Mathematical Domain Discrete point on \(S^2\) Continuous low-frequency function over \(S^2\)
Light Type Direct, sharp, localized illumination Diffuse, indirect, ambient/environmental illumination
Shader Workload 1 dot product, 1 clamp Quadratic polynomial evaluation using normal components
High Frequencies Yes (hard shadows, sharp specular highlights) No (inherently blurred/low-frequency, no specular)

4. Implementation in Three.js Shaders

A directional vector updates diffuse lighting linearly per light source:

// Directional light evaluation
float dotNL = clamp(dot(geometryNormal, directionalLight.direction), 0.0, 1.0);
vec3 directDiffuse = dotNL * directionalLight.color;

A spherical harmonics probe evaluates irradiance as a continuous field without tracing multiple light rays:

// Spherical harmonics diffuse evaluation (Three.js LightProbe)
vec3 irradiance = shGetIrradianceAt(geometryNormal, sphericalHarmonicsCoefficients);
vec3 indirectDiffuse = irradiance * diffuseColor;

Directional vectors provide computationally cheap, high-frequency directional lighting suitable for the sun or localized spots. Spherical harmonics provide a compressed representation of low-frequency diffuse ambient light from an entire environment map, bypassing texture lookups and hemispherical numerical integration.
