How Three.js PMREMGenerator Filters Cubemaps

This article provides an in-depth breakdown of how the PMREMGenerator in Three.js processes, filters, and packages environment maps to deliver physically accurate, roughness-dependent specular reflections. It covers the underlying mathematics of microfacet BRDFs, the split-sum approximation, the iterative multi-pass convolution pipeline, seam-mitigation strategies, and how the final packed atlas is consumed by physically based rendering (PBR) materials at runtime.

The Role of PMREM in Specular Image-Based Lighting

In physically based rendering, calculating the specular reflection of an environment on a rough surface requires solving the rendering equation across the specular lobe. For a microfacet model using the GGX/Trowbridge-Reitz normal distribution function (NDF), this entails integrating the incoming radiance weighted by the BRDF over the hemisphere:

\[L_o(v) = \int_{\Omega} L_i(l) f(l, v) (n \cdot l) dl\]

Evaluating this integral per-pixel in real time via Monte Carlo integration is computationally prohibitive on modern GPUs. Three.js addresses this using the split-sum approximation popularized by Epic Games. The split-sum formulation separates the integral into two independent components:

  1. The Pre-Integrated BRDF (Environment BRDF): A 2D lookup table (LUT) parameterized by the cosine of the viewing angle (\(\cos \theta\)) and surface roughness.
  2. The Filtered Radiance (Pre-filtered Environment Map): The environment map convolved with the GGX NDF across various roughness values.

The PMREMGenerator (Prefiltered Mipmapped Radiance Environment Map Generator) is responsible for pre-computing the second part of this equation.

Input Ingestion and Format Standardization

The PMREMGenerator accepts various input sources, including equirectangular textures, cube textures, and dynamic 3D scenes rendered from a central viewpoint via a CubeCamera.

When an input texture is passed to the generator via .fromEquirectangular() or .fromCubemap(), the generator ensures the data is mapped to a standardized floating-point representation. Equirectangular textures are projected onto a standard cubemap or internal render targets using a dedicated conversion shader that samples the equirectangular projection spherically:

\[u = \frac{\text{atan2}(z, x)}{2\pi} + 0.5, \quad v = \frac{\arcsin(y)}{\pi} + 0.5\]

Dynamic ranges are preserved by utilizing HalfFloatType (RGBA16F) or FloatType (RGBA32F) render buffers, ensuring high-dynamic-range (HDR) data avoids clamping prior to convolution.

Iterative Filtering and GGX Convolution Pipeline

Standard mipmapping applies a simple box or Gaussian filter down the mip chain, which averages texels linearly. In contrast, PMREM generation requires filtering the environment using an angular distribution defined by the GGX microfacet model, where each consecutive level represents an increasing roughness value.

Roughness-to-Mip Mapping

The generator constructs a fixed sequence of mip levels. Rather than linearly incrementing roughness with mip levels, Three.js maps the perceptual roughness parameter \(\alpha = \text{roughness}^2\) across the levels:

  • Level 0 (Base Resolution): Near-zero roughness (\(\alpha \approx 0\)). This captures mirror-like reflections and represents the sharpest capture of the environment.
  • Intermediate Levels: Successively higher roughness values convolved with wider GGX lobes.
  • Final Level: Fully diffuse or maximum specular roughness (\(\alpha = 1.0\)), capturing extremely broad, blurry ambient reflections.

Progressive Filtering

Convolving the original highest-resolution map for every individual roughness tier using a large sample count is slow. To optimize performance, the PMREMGenerator implements a progressive downsampling approach.

For each level \(N\), the shader samples from level \(N - 1\) rather than repeatedly evaluating the raw base texture. Because high roughness values act as low-pass filters that remove high-frequency details, sampling from an already convolved, lower-resolution representation maintains visual accuracy while dramatically reducing the sampling radius and texture bandwidth required.

Importance Sampling and Quasi-Monte Carlo Weights

Within the convolution pass shader, the generator executes an approximation of importance sampling over the GGX distribution. Direction vectors for reflection samples are calculated using a low-discrepancy sequence (typically Hammersley sequences or spherical Fibonacci distributions). Each sample direction is evaluated based on:

  • The half-vector \(H\) generated from the sequence and the roughness \(\alpha\).
  • The reflection vector \(L = 2(V \cdot H)H - V\).
  • The solid angle associated with each sample relative to the texel solid angle, which dictates the implicit mip-level to fetch from to minimize aliasing.

The Packed 2D Render Target Layout

Standard cube-map render targets natively require six distinct faces, which can introduce pipeline stalls due to frequent frame buffer binding swaps or driver overhead in WebGL. Furthermore, sampling across cube seams during blur passes frequently yields visible edge artifacts.

To overcome these constraints, PMREMGenerator processes and packs the entire mip chain into a single 2D atlas texture:

  1. Octahedral Projection or Face Packing: The cubemap faces are unrolled or packed into a contiguous 2D texture coordinate space.
  2. Mip Strip Arrangement: The base mip level occupies the largest portion of the atlas (e.g., a \(256 \times 256\) block). Successive mip levels are placed alongside or below each other in a structured grid, shrinking by a power of two at each step.
  3. Padding and Edge Clamping: To prevent bilinear filtering and sampling bleeding across adjacent faces or differing mip levels, border margins (padding texels) are generated around each sub-face. The padding copies the neighboring spherical coordinates from adjacent cubemap faces, guaranteeing smooth transitions across 90-degree cube boundaries.

Consumption in Shaders

Once the PMREMGenerator finishes its render passes, it returns a WebGLRenderTarget containing the packed radiance map.

When a PBR material such as MeshStandardMaterial or MeshPhysicalMaterial renders:

  1. The material's shader calculates the reflection vector \(R\) from the surface normal \(N\) and view vector \(V\).
  2. It reads the surface's perceptual roughness and converts it to the matching fractional mip level in the PMREM atlas.
  3. The shader evaluates the custom coordinate transformations to translate the spherical reflection vector \(R\) into the UV coordinates of the specific mip strip in the 2D packed atlas.
  4. It performs a trilinear fetch (sampling between two adjacent mip levels in the atlas and interpolating the results) to obtain the prefiltered radiance \(L_i\).
  5. This radiance is multiplied by the pre-integrated environment BRDF term (derived analytically or fetched from an internal LUT) and modulated by the Fresnel reflectance to yield the final specular contribution.
