Why Three.js Quaternions Require Vector4 Math

This article explores the mathematical necessity of utilizing a four-dimensional representation, such as a Vector4, when handling complex quaternion calculations in Three.js. While 3D space involves only three spatial dimensions, orientation cannot be represented continuously and without singularities using only three scalar parameters. The following breakdown explains why quaternions inherently belong to four-dimensional space (\(\mathbb{R}^4\)), how vector rotation relies on four-part hypercomplex conjugation, and why Three.js relies on four-component structures to execute reliable, gimbal-lock-free 3D transformations.

The Mathematical Structure of Quaternions

A quaternion \(q\) is an element of the four-dimensional normed division algebra \(\mathbb{H}\). Mathematically, it is defined as:

\[q = w + xi + yj + zk\]

where \(w, x, y, z \in \mathbb{R}\), and \(i, j, k\) are imaginary units satisfying Hamilton's fundamental identities:

\[i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1\]

A quaternion consists of a scalar part (\(w\)) and a three-dimensional vector part (\(\mathbf{v} = xi + yj + zk\)). Reducing this structure to a three-component vector (Vector3) discards one of these components, breaking the algebraic closure required for quaternion multiplication.

When expressing a 3D rotation by an angle \(\theta\) around a normalized unit axis \(\mathbf{u} = (u_x, u_y, u_z)\), the unit quaternion is defined as:

\[q = \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) + \mathbf{u}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \left(u_x\sin\frac{\theta}{2}, u_y\sin\frac{\theta}{2}, u_z\sin\frac{\theta}{2}, \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\]

This encoding explicitly demands four distinct scalar values: three to scale the unit axis vector by \(\sin(\theta/2)\), and a fourth independent scalar to encode \(\cos(\theta/2)\). A Vector3 cannot hold both the directional vector and the scalar half-angle simultaneously without loss of data.

Pure Quaternions and Vector Conjugation

To rotate a 3D vector \(\mathbf{p} = (p_x, p_y, p_z)\) using a quaternion in Three.js, the vector must undergo quaternion conjugation:

\[\mathbf{p}' = q \mathbf{p} q^{-1}\]

This operation is impossible using standard vector operations in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). For this formula to be mathematically valid, the vector \(\mathbf{p}\) must be embedded into \(\mathbb{H}\) as a "pure quaternion" whose real component is zero:

\[\mathbf{p}_{\mathbb{H}} = 0 + p_x i + p_y j + p_z k\]

In computational terms, this requires representing the 3D point as a four-component vector \((p_x, p_y, p_z, 0)\). The calculation involves two hypercomplex multiplications. Discarding the fourth component prevents the formation of the inverse \(q^{-1}\) (or conjugate \(q^*\)) and corrupts the cross-product and dot-product interactions that occur during the Hamilton product expansion:

\[q_1 q_2 = (w_1 w_2 - \mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2, \, w_1 \mathbf{v}_2 + w_2 \mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_1 \times \mathbf{v}_2)\]

Without the fourth component, the inner product term \(\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2\) and the real-part scaling cannot be resolved.

Topological Necessity: The Unit 3-Sphere (\(S^3\))

Rotations in three dimensions belong to the Special Orthogonal group \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\). The topology of \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\) is homeomorphic to real projective space \(\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^3\).

Any continuous three-parameter representation of \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\)—such as Euler angles (Vector3) or Rodrigues vectors—either contains coordinate singularities (gimbal lock) or discontinuities at specific boundary points. According to topology, \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\) cannot be globally parameterized by a subset of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) without introducing singularities.

Unit quaternions solve this by living on the unit 3-sphere (\(S^3\)), which is embedded in four-dimensional Euclidean space (\(\mathbb{R}^4\)):

\[x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = 1\]

The 3-sphere \(S^3\) acts as a double cover of \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\), where each rotation corresponds to an antipodal pair of unit quaternions (\(q\) and \(-q\)). Navigating this space continuously—such as performing Spherical Linear Interpolation (slerp) between orientations—strictly requires interpolating along geodesics in \(\mathbb{R}^4\). A three-component format cannot preserve the constraint \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = 1\).

Computational Alignment in Three.js and WebGL

Beyond pure mathematics, Three.js coordinates quaternion data directly with the GPU through WebGL shaders. In linear algebra pipelines:

  1. Memory Alignment: A quaternion maps directly to a 4-component floating-point vector (vec4 in GLSL or Vector4 in JavaScript).
  2. Matrix Conversion: Converting a quaternion into a \(4 \times 4\) homogeneous transformation matrix requires calculating quadratic terms from all four coordinates (\(x^2, y^2, z^2, w^2, xy, xz, yz, wx, wy, wz\)). Omitting the \(w\) component prevents the reconstruction of the off-diagonal shear and rotational terms.
  3. Interpolation Arithmetic: The 4D dot product \(q_1 \cdot q_2 = x_1 x_2 + y_1 y_2 + z_1 z_2 + w_1 w_2\) determines the cosine of the angle between two orientations on \(S^3\), which dictates whether the shortest path interpolation requires negating one quaternion.

Because the underlying algebra, topological space, and hardware execution models all depend on four orthogonal components, utilizing a four-dimensional vector structure is an explicit mathematical requirement for quaternion mechanics.
