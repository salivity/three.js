Fixing Three.js Color Banding with dithering_fragment

Color banding is a common visual artifact in WebGL rendering that disrupts smooth gradients with harsh, visible steps, particularly in low-contrast areas and soft shadows. This article examines the visual purpose of the dithering_fragment chunk in Three.js, explaining how it alters the final pixel output to trick the human eye, disperse quantization boundaries, and restore the appearance of smooth tonal transitions on standard 8-bit displays.

The Cause of Color Banding

Color banding occurs primarily due to limitations in display bit depth. Most standard digital displays render color using 8 bits per channel (24-bit RGB), providing only 256 discrete levels of intensity for red, green, and blue. When a scene contains subtle lighting gradients, expansive atmospheric fog, or soft shadow penumbras, the difference in calculated color values between adjacent pixels is often smaller than the smallest representable step in an 8-bit color space.

As a result, multiple consecutive pixels are rounded (quantized) to the exact same color value before abruptly jumping to the next available value. Visually, this manifests as stair-stepped, concentric lines or stripes across what should be a seamless, photorealistic gradient.

The Role and Visual Purpose of dithering_fragment

The dithering_fragment chunk is a specialized GLSL shader module included within Three.js standard materials. Its primary visual purpose is to replace distinct, low-frequency banding edges with imperceptible, high-frequency noise patterns.

Instead of allowing colors to be uniformly rounded down across wide areas, dithering_fragment adds a tiny, deterministic, screen-space pseudo-random offset to the final computed color of each fragment immediately before output quantization. When the color values are converted to 8-bit integers, this offset causes pixels near the transition border between two discrete color steps to probabilistically round up or down.

Visually, this transforms sharp, linear boundaries into a fine, speckled distribution of adjacent color shades. Because the human visual system inherently acts as a low-pass filter, the eye blends these alternating pixels together over spatial distance. The viewer perceives a continuous, smooth gradation rather than distinct, stepping bands.

Implementation in Three.js

Three.js abstracts the manual inclusion of the dithering_fragment chunk through a simple material property. Setting material.dithering = true on supported materials—such as MeshStandardMaterial, MeshPhysicalMaterial, or MeshBasicMaterial—injects this chunk into the material's fragment shader at compile time.

By applying spatial noise at the sub-perceptual level, dithering_fragment provides an efficient, hardware-friendly solution that significantly enhances final render quality without requiring expensive post-processing passes or native 10-bit/16-bit rendering pipelines.
