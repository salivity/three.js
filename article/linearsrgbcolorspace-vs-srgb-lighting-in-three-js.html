LinearSRGBColorSpace vs sRGB Lighting in Three.js

This article explains the mechanical and visual differences between LinearSRGBColorSpace and standard sRGB when rendering light in Three.js. It details why physically accurate lighting math requires linear calculations, how gamma-encoded colors distort light attenuation, and how Three.js implements these color spaces to produce realistic scenes without banding or unnatural falloff.

Understanding the Color Spaces

The standard sRGB color space is non-linear. It applies a transfer function (gamma curve of approximately 2.2) to match the non-linear way human eyes perceive brightness in dark and mid-tone ranges. This curve optimizes image storage by allocating more data bits to darker values where the human eye is most sensitive.

In contrast, LinearSRGBColorSpace represents light intensities on a strictly linear scale. In a linear space, doubling the numerical value of a pixel doubles the amount of physical light it represents (\(1.0 + 1.0 = 2.0\)).

The Problem with Lighting in sRGB

Lighting models—including basic Lambertian diffuse, Blinn-Phong, and advanced physically based rendering (PBR)—are derived from the physical laws of optics. These mathematical models rely on linear algebra and assume that light values combine additively.

When lighting calculations occur directly within non-linear sRGB:

  • Falloff is Distorted: Light attenuation equations (such as the inverse-square law) drop off too steeply. Shadows become excessively harsh, and mid-tones collapse into darkness.
  • Overblown Highlights: Adding multiple light sources in non-linear space creates artificial clipping and blown-out highlights rather than natural illumination.
  • Color Shifts: Mixing colors non-linearly skews chromaticity. For example, a red light overlapping a green surface will produce a muddy brown edge rather than a clean yellow transition.

How Three.js Utilizes LinearSRGBColorSpace

Modern Three.js enforces a linear-first rendering workflow to maintain physical accuracy:

  1. Input Textures: Standard textures (diffuse, albedo) are usually encoded in sRGB. When imported, they must be flagged using texture.colorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace. Three.js automatically converts these textures to linear space upon sampling in the shader. Data textures (normal maps, roughness maps) remain linear.
  2. Lighting Math: Three.js performs all light interaction, shading, and blending strictly in LinearSRGBColorSpace. This guarantees that light addition, shadows, and reflection vectors behave according to real-world physics.
  3. Display Output: Human monitors expect an sRGB signal. Once the lighting calculations are complete, Three.js encodes the final rendered frame into the display format via renderer.outputColorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace.

Visual Results

Using LinearSRGBColorSpace for lighting calculations produces smooth, realistic gradients across curved geometries, accurate shadows that do not crush detail in the mid-tones, and natural color mixing between lights and materials. Calculating lighting in raw sRGB produces an unnatural high-contrast appearance that cannot be corrected by simply adjusting light intensity.
