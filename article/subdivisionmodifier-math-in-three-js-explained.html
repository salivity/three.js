SubdivisionModifier Math in Three.js Explained

This article explores the mathematical mechanics behind the SubdivisionModifier in Three.js, detailing how it converts low-polygon geometries into smooth, high-resolution surfaces. At its core, the modifier relies on Charles Loop’s subdivision scheme, an approximating surface algorithm designed specifically for triangular meshes. By exploring the topological splitting process, edge-point calculation masks, and existing vertex repositioning formulas, this guide clarifies the exact linear algebraic functions that generate curvature and surface continuity.

The Foundation: Loop Subdivision Scheme

The SubdivisionModifier in Three.js operates on triangular meshes using the Loop subdivision algorithm. This process is both topological and geometric: it increases mesh density by subdividing faces and repositions vertices via discrete weighted averaging functions to converge toward a smooth parametric limit surface.

The algorithm executes two primary phases during each subdivision pass:

  1. Topological Refinement (Splitting): Each triangular face is split into four smaller sub-triangles by introducing a new vertex along every edge (a 1-to-4 split).
  2. Geometric Smoothing (Averaging): The spatial coordinates of both the newly created vertices and the original vertices are computed as weighted linear combinations of their immediate neighbors.

1. Generating New Edge Vertices (Odd Vertices)

When an edge is split, the position of the newly introduced vertex \(v_{\text{edge}}\) depends on the two endpoints of the edge and the two opposing vertices of the adjacent triangles sharing that edge.

For an interior edge bounded by endpoints \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), and flanked by opposing vertices \(v_3\) and \(v_4\), the position is defined by the following weighted affine combination:

\[v_{\text{edge}} = \frac{3}{8}(v_1 + v_2) + \frac{1}{8}(v_3 + v_4)\]

This mask applies strong geometric tension to the original edge segment (\(\frac{6}{8}\) total weight) while pulling the new point slightly toward the flanking faces (\(\frac{2}{8}\) total weight), introducing local curvature.

If the edge lies on an open mesh boundary (where only one adjacent triangle exists), the opposing vertices do not exist. In this case, a boundary subdivision rule is applied to preserve silhouette integrity:

\[v_{\text{edge, boundary}} = \frac{1}{2}(v_1 + v_2)\]

2. Updating Existing Vertices (Even Vertices)

Existing vertices are not kept in their original positions; doing so would produce an interpolating, faceted shape rather than a smooth surface. Instead, each original vertex \(v\) is repositioned using a convex combination of its current position and the positions of all directly adjacent neighboring vertices \(v_i\).

For a vertex with valence \(n\) (the number of connected edges):

\[v' = (1 - n\beta)v + \beta \sum_{i=1}^{n} v_i\]

Where:

  • \(v\) is the current position vector of the vertex.
  • \(v'\) is the updated position vector.
  • \(n\) is the vertex valence.
  • \(v_i\) represents the position vectors of the \(n\) adjacent neighbor vertices.
  • \(\beta\) is a weighting coefficient chosen to ensure numerical stability and continuous curvature.

Calculating the Parameter \(\beta\)

Charles Loop derived \(\beta\) using spectral analysis of the subdivision matrix to ensure smooth surface properties regardless of valence:

\[\beta = \frac{1}{n} \left( \frac{5}{8} - \left( \frac{3}{8} + \frac{1}{4} \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right) \right)^2 \right)\]

Many implementations, including optimized real-time variations, adopt Joe Warren’s simplified formulation for computational efficiency:

\[\beta = \begin{cases} \frac{3}{16}, & \text{if } n = 3 \\ \frac{3}{8n}, & \text{if } n > 3 \end{cases}\]

Boundary Rule for Existing Vertices

If an existing vertex lies along an open mesh boundary with two boundary neighbor vertices \(v_{\text{b1}}\) and \(v_{\text{b2}}\), its position is smoothed using a one-dimensional cubic B-spline mask:

\[v'_{\text{boundary}} = \frac{3}{4}v + \frac{1}{8}(v_{\text{b1}} + v_{\text{b2}})\]

Mathematical Properties and Limit Surface

By repeatedly applying these linear transformations, the geometry converges to a well-defined limit surface:

  • Curvature Continuity: The limit surface achieves \(C^2\) continuity (continuous curvature) across all regular regions (vertices of valence 6). At extraordinary vertices (vertices where \(n \neq 6\)), the surface maintains \(C^1\) continuity (tangent plane continuity).
  • Affine Invariance: Because all subdivision weights sum to 1 (\(\sum w = 1\)), the geometric transformations are independent of the coordinate system origin, ensuring predictable behavior under translation, rotation, and scaling.
  • Approximation Behavior: The surface contracts slightly relative to the convex hull of the control mesh, smoothing sharp peaks into rounded profiles while converging to a continuous spline-like representation.
